02 / CLASSICAL CONDITIONING

Pavlov: learning an association

What’s sometimes called “Pavlov’s law” is usually taught as classical conditioning. Ivan Pavlov found that dogs could learn to salivate to a signal repeatedly paired with food.

BEFORE LEARNING

Food → salivation

Food is the unconditioned stimulus; salivation is the unconditioned response.

DURING LEARNING

Signal + food

A previously neutral signal repeatedly predicts food.

AFTER LEARNING

Signal → salivation

The signal becomes a conditioned stimulus; salivation to it is the conditioned response.

Learning the association is acquisition. Repeatedly presenting the signal without food can weaken the learned response: extinction. A single missing reward in our activity does not demonstrate extinction.

Source: OpenStax Psychology 2e, §6.2

03 / OPERANT CONDITIONING

Learning from consequences

Associated with B. F. Skinner, operant conditioning changes behavior through its consequences. Reinforcement increases a behavior; punishment decreases it. “Positive” means adding something, and “negative” means removing something.

Four ways consequences can influence behavior
TypeWhat changes?Example, if it has that effect
Positive reinforcementAdd something; behavior increases.Praise after studying encourages more studying.
Negative reinforcementRemove something; behavior increases.Buckling a seat belt stops a beep, encouraging buckling.
Positive punishmentAdd something; behavior decreases.Extra chores after breaking a rule reduce rule-breaking.
Negative punishmentRemove something; behavior decreases.Losing game time reduces rule-breaking.

Source: OpenStax Psychology 2e, §6.3

THE KEY DIFFERENCE

A signal predicts. A consequence shapes.

In Pavlov’s experiments, a signal predicted food. In this activity, points depended on clicking. Classical conditioning links stimuli; operant conditioning links behavior with its consequences.
